  Balkh was once one of the most important cities in the ancient world — the Bactrian capital that Alexander the Great made his base for the eastern campaigns, a centre of Zoroastrianism before Islam, and later a hub of Buddhist learning. The Arab geographers called it "umm al-bilad" — the Mother of Cities. Ancient Balkh — known to the Greeks as Bactra — lies 24 kilometres northwest of Mazar-i-Sharif, a short drive through agricultural flat land. The site is dominated by the massive earthen ramparts of the ancient city walls, which once enclosed an urban area more than 11 kilometres in circumference. Within and around the walls are the remains of mosques, mausoleums, and fortifications from different periods — including a mosque believed to date to the early Islamic conquest.

The modern village of Balkh sits amid the ruins, giving the site a lived-in quality that purely archaeological sites lack. The 15th-century Green Mosque (Masjid-i-Sabz), tiled in the same turquoise that characterises Timurid architecture throughout Central Asia, is worth seeking out within the ruins. The overall impression of Balkh is of a site whose significance has dramatically outgrown whatever is currently visible — it rewards visitors who come with some knowledge of what once stood here.
